💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
barbed wire (BARBD WYR): Fence wire with sharp points twisted into it, used to keep cattle in or out.
corporation (kor-puh-RAY-shun): A business owned by many investors, who share its profits and risks.
overgrazing (oh-ver-GRAY-zing): Letting too many animals eat the grass until it cannot grow back.
regulate (REG-yoo-layt): To control something by rules and laws.
syndicate (SIN-duh-kit): A group of investors who join together to carry out one business.
wage (WAYJ): Money paid to a worker for work done, usually by the day, week, or month.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
For a few years, one cowboy could ride a hundred miles and never meet a fence. Then a twisted piece of wire changed the plains.
The open range did not last. Within a generation, great ranches were fenced, bought by companies, and sometimes plowed under. You will meet the cattle barons who built huge ranches in Texas, see what barbed wire did, and see how the ranching world changed for the people who worked in it.

📚 PART 1 · THE BARONS

The Cattle Barons

The cattle drives made a few ranchers very rich. People called them cattle barons. Several of them began before the Civil War, and they grew with the markets that came after it.

Richard King started the King Ranch in South Texas in 1853. He was a former steamboat captain. He built it into one of the largest ranches in the world, and it still covers more than 800,000 acres. Charles Goodnight and Oliver Loving drove cattle west and north in 1866 along what became the Goodnight-Loving Trail, to feed soldiers and Native people on reservations in New Mexico, and to find new markets in Colorado. Loving was wounded in a fight with Comanche raiders on the way, and he died. Goodnight carried his friend’s body hundreds of miles home to Weatherford for burial, as he had promised.

Goodnight kept using the trail after Loving’s death. Near Raton Pass, a man known as Uncle Dick Wootton ran a toll station, where travelers paid a fee to pass, and he charged Goodnight ten cents a head for his cattle. Goodnight paid, but not without protest, and he later opened a new route through Tinchera Pass to avoid it. In 1868 he agreed to deliver cattle to a rancher named John Wesley Iliff at Cheyenne, Wyoming, a town on the Union Pacific Railroad. Cattlemen from Texas, New Mexico, and Colorado used all or part of the trail until railroads reached the Southwest in the early 1880s.

Meanwhile, in South Texas, Richard King’s ranch kept growing. King and a partner set up a cattle camp on Santa Gertrudis Creek in 1852, and in 1853 they bought a Spanish land grant of 15,500 acres there. Later, King’s old steamboat partner, Mifflin Kenedy, bought a share in the ranch, and the two men went their separate ways in 1868. That same year King fenced a tract around his headquarters. On a cattle-buying trip in Mexico, he met about one hundred men, women, and children and brought them north to help tend his herds. They became known as los Kineños, or “King’s men.” King died in 1885, and his wife, Henrietta, asked Robert Kleberg, the ranch’s lawyer, to manage it.

John Chisum, who came to North Texas as a boy, built a herd of tens of thousands along the Pecos River in New Mexico. He is a different man from Jesse Chisholm, for whom the Chisholm Trail was named. W. T. Waggoner built a ranch in North Texas that grew to over half a million acres. In 1911, workers on the Waggoner Ranch drilled for water and found oil instead. C. C. Slaughter ran cattle on the South Plains and became a Dallas banker. These ranchers took risks and worked hard. They also had the good fortune of cheap land and a growing nation that wanted beef.

📚 PART 2 · THE WIRE

The Wire and the Windmill

In 1874, an Illinois farmer named Joseph Glidden patented a new kind of fence: wire twisted with sharp barbs. It was cheap, strong, and easy to build. A story says that in 1876, a salesman fenced a pen of wild Texas longhorns in a plaza in San Antonio to show that the wire could hold them. Whether or not it happened just that way, ranchers soon saw that barbed wire would let them keep cattle on their own land, and keep other herds off it.

Wire spread quickly because there was little good fencing material on the plains. The most popular fence in Texas was a hedge of thorny bois d’arc trees. Glidden’s wire, which wrapped sharp barbs around one strand and held them in place with a twist of a second strand, was the most successful of hundreds of designs. In 1876 he sold his interests to a Massachusetts wire company, which soon held nearly all the major barbed-wire patents. After that demonstration in San Antonio, sales grew fast.

Open-range ranchers hated it. Fences cut off trails, and cut off water holes that had been free to everyone. In 1883, in a drought year, angry cowboys and small ranchers began to cut fences across Texas. The trouble spread to dozens of counties. People were hurt and killed. In 1884, the legislature made fence cutting a felony, and it also required fence builders to put in gates. The open range was ending.

Not all the fencing was on a rancher’s own land. Most ranchers fenced land they owned or leased, but some put wire around public land, and some strung it around farms that belonged to other people. Fences blocked public roads, and a few cut off schools and churches or got in the way of the mail. At night, armed bands with names like the Owls, the Javelinas, and the Blue Devils cut the wire. Fence cutting was reported in more than half the counties in Texas. At least three men were killed, and by the fall of 1883 the damage was estimated at $20 million. In the town of Henrietta, spokesmen for the cutters met with ranchers from Clay County. They agreed that fences would come down across public roads, that farmers would get gates, and that the cutting would stop.

Texas historian Walter Prescott Webb, who was born in East Texas in 1888 and taught at the University of Texas, later wrote that three tools made it possible to settle the dry Great Plains: the Colt revolver, barbed wire, and the windmill. Each did a different job. The revolver helped defend against attack, the wire marked ownership, and the windmill gave water. Webb’s idea is a good reminder that machines can change a landscape, though no one tool does it alone.

Fences and wells changed the land itself. Windmills let cattle graze where there had been no water, and herds grew. Many ranchers put more cattle on the range than it could feed. This overgrazing tore up the grass, and mesquite brush spread across ground where grass had been thick. A mid-1880s drought and a fall in cattle prices ruined many ranchers who had borrowed to buy too many animals.

Daniel Halladay built the first American windmill, in Connecticut in 1854. It had a tail, which Texas cowhands called a vane, that turned the wheel into the wind, so it ran without anyone watching. Windmills came to the ranches along with wire. When ranchers fenced the springs and creeks, the land behind the fences had no water, and some ranchers began to drill wells. In 1882, in a drought, a sheepman named Christopher Doty drilled a fifty-two-foot well north of Eldorado and put up a windmill, and it watered his 4,000 head of stock. By 1900 the XIT Ranch alone had 335 windmills, and windmills were a common sight across Texas. Drillers followed the fence crews, windmillers followed the drillers, and range riders climbed the towers to grease the wheels twice a week.

📚 PART 3 · A NEW BUSINESS

Ranching Becomes a Business

Ranching now needed more money than most families had. Land, fences, windmills, and a large herd all cost a lot. So corporations moved in. Investors in Chicago, Scotland, and England put their money into Texas ranches. In 1879, the Matador Ranch began in the Panhandle, and in 1882 it was sold to a Scottish company. The biggest of all was the XIT. The Texas government had no money to pay for a new state capitol, so it traded three million acres of Panhandle land to a Chicago group in exchange for building one. The group turned the land into a ranch that stretched across ten counties.

The Matador shows how this worked. A Texas cattleman named Hank Campbell started it in 1878 along the Pease River in Motley County, with a banker as his partner. In 1882, the banker went to Dundee, Scotland, and found businessmen who wanted to invest. They formed a company and bought the ranch for $1,250,000. The price covered about 100,000 acres, the right to range over 1,500,000 more, and 40,000 cattle. Campbell stayed on as superintendent, and the new owners put up fences, windmills, and water tanks.

The XIT deal became urgent after a fire. In 1879 the legislature had set aside the three million acres to pay for a new capitol, and when the old capitol burned on November 9, 1881, building the new one could not wait. A contractor agreed to build it in return for the land, and a Chicago company soon took over most of his deal and formed the Capitol syndicate. The land lay in the unsettled Panhandle, so the syndicate started a ranch to use it until it could be sold. In late 1884, one of its leaders, John Farwell, went to England and raised about five million dollars from British investors. The ranch finished its first fenced pasture in 1885. The capitol itself was completed in April 1888 and cost about $3.7 million.

The cowboy changed too. In the days of the drives, many cowboys hoped to start herds of their own. By the 1880s, ranches were big companies, and a cowboy worked for a wage, usually about $25 to $40 a month. In 1883, dozens of cowboys in the Panhandle went on strike for higher pay. The ranch owners refused, and the strike failed. The cowboy’s life was still hard, but now he worked for someone else.

The strike grew out of these changes. Before, a cowboy could take part of his pay in calves, brand a maverick, or even run a few cattle of his own on his employer’s land. The new owners wanted hands to work only for wages, and they claimed every maverick for the company. In the late winter of 1883, crews from three ranches, the LIT, the LS, and the LX, wrote a list of demands for higher wages. Twenty-four men signed it and set March 31 as the day to stop work. A cowboy named Tom Harris of the LS led them. They started a small strike fund, and newspapers as far away as Colorado reported the news. The strikers promised a peaceful protest, and there was no violence. Two ranches fired striking hands at once. Two others offered a slight raise and fired anyone who refused it. After two and a half months the strike had weakened, and the spring roundup went on without trouble.

Government Steps In

Texans began to ask whether companies from far away should own so much of the state. In 1891, Governor James Stephen Hogg signed a law meant to keep foreign companies from holding Texas land. The same year, Texas created the Railroad Commission to regulate what railroads could charge, since ranchers and farmers depended on them. It was the beginning of a regulatory era in Texas.

Hogg came to the governor’s office with a record. As attorney general, he had gone to court to return more than a million and a half acres of public land to the state, and he had broken up a group of railroads that agreed together on rates. He decided that the legislature and his small staff could not control powerful railroad companies by themselves, so he asked for a Railroad Commission and won the race for governor in 1890 on that promise. Hogg’s laws also required land companies to sell their holdings within fifteen years. The alien land law passed in April 1891, but a court struck it down that December on a technical point, and a new law in 1892 left out the ban on foreign companies.

Meanwhile, settlers kept coming. Railroads sold land to farmers, and new towns grew up. Farmers plowed the grass where ranches had been and planted wheat and cotton. For a time, the rains came and the crops grew. But the grass had held the soil in place for thousands of years. When it was gone and the rains stopped, the wind would take the soil away. Texans of a later generation would pay for that.

The plowed grass would matter later. On the southern plains, droughts come regularly, with a very bad one about every twenty years and milder ones every three or four. In the 1930s, a long drought, high heat, and strong winds struck western Texas and the lands around it, and great dust storms followed. The worst year in Amarillo was 1935, when the storms lasted a total of 908 hours. At its largest that year, the area called the Dust Bowl covered 100 million acres.
